## 2.1 REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

### 2.1.1 EVOLUTION OF DIGITAL DOCUMENT STORAGE

#### 2.1.1.1 FROM PHYSICAL ARCHIVES TO DIGITAL DOCUMENT MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS
The transition from physical to digital document storage marks a major development in modern information management. Traditional paper-based systems were limited by storage capacity, slow retrieval processes, and vulnerability to physical damage. To address these limitations, organizations adopted Digital Document Management Systems (DMS) that enable electronic storage, structured organization, and efficient retrieval of documents through indexing and search mechanisms [46]–[48].

Studies on digital document management highlight several advantages over traditional archives. Digital systems improve document accessibility, support remote collaboration, and reduce operational costs associated with physical storage and manual document handling. They also enable faster information retrieval through metadata tagging and keyword indexing, allowing organizations to manage large volumes of information more efficiently [47], [49]. In addition, automated workflows integrated into digital archives further enhance document processing, classification, and retrieval efficiency compared with manual record-keeping systems [46], [47].

#### 2.1.1.2 CLOUD-BASED DOCUMENT STORAGE
The evolution of digital storage was further accelerated by the emergence of cloud computing, which significantly transformed how documents are stored, accessed, and shared. According to the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), cloud computing is defined as a model that enables ubiquitous, on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources such as servers, storage, applications, and services that can be rapidly provisioned with minimal management effort [49].

Cloud storage platforms provide several advantages over traditional on-premise systems. Research highlights scalability, remote accessibility, and collaborative capabilities as primary factors driving cloud adoption. Organizations can dynamically adjust storage resources according to demand while allowing users to access documents from any internet-connected device. Cloud repositories also enable centralized storage and real-time collaboration, allowing distributed teams to share and co-edit documents more efficiently [51], [52].

### 2.1.2 CLOUD-BASED STORAGE AND SECURITY CHALLENGES
Despite its benefits, cloud computing introduces significant security challenges. Storing data in third-party infrastructures reduces direct organizational control over physical storage resources and expands the potential attack surface. Research identifies several risks affecting cloud-based document storage, including misconfigurations, unauthorized access, and vulnerabilities associated with shared infrastructure and multi-tenant environments [53]. These security concerns highlight the need for stronger data protection mechanisms when adopting cloud-based document storage systems, particularly in environments where sensitive or confidential information is involved [97] , [121] .

Cloud storage services such as Google Drive, iCloud, Dropbox, and Microsoft OneDrive are widely used due to their accessibility and integrated security features, including encryption, authentication, and access control mechanisms [97]. These platforms enable users to store, synchronize, and share documents conveniently across devices and locations. However, security evaluations and academic studies reveal persistent vulnerabilities that may expose stored data to potential threats [97], [122].

Research conducted by Sultan Majid Alshamsi highlights that cloud storage providers may remain vulnerable to OAuth misconfigurations, API exposures, and weaknesses in authentication flows, indicating that data stored in these platforms can still be at risk despite standard security measures [54]. Additional security analyses emphasize that improper encryption implementation, delayed system patching, inadequate access control policies, and insufficient monitoring mechanisms may lead to unintended data exposure or security breaches [55].

Furthermore, studies on man-in-the-cloud attack techniques demonstrate that compromised authentication tokens can allow attackers to gain unauthorized access to cloud accounts without stealing traditional login credentials. Forensic investigations also reveal the presence of residual artifacts on local devices, challenging assumptions regarding complete data isolation in cloud storage environments. User-trust surveys similarly indicate that concerns regarding privacy and unauthorized access remain prevalent even when cloud services provide built-in encryption and security controls [56], [57].

Traditional cloud security frameworks largely rely on encryption and perimeter protection. However, once encrypted files are exfiltrated from the storage environment, the protection shifts from system boundaries to the encrypted data itself, exposing limitations in conventional security strategies. Studies on cloud storage security emphasize that encryption alone cannot guarantee comprehensive protection, particularly when infrastructure configurations, access controls, or key management practices are inadequate. As a result, researchers highlight the need for additional security mechanisms to complement encryption in order to effectively protect sensitive data stored in cloud environments [58].

### 2.1.3 ENCRYPTION AS A FOUNDATIONAL SECURITY MECHANISM

#### 2.1.3.1 ADVANCED ENCRYPTION STANDARD (AES) FOR DATA PROTECTION
Encryption remains a fundamental mechanism for protecting digital information, particularly in cloud-based environments where sensitive data is transmitted and stored across distributed infrastructures. Among modern encryption standards, the Advanced Encryption Standard is widely recognized as one of the most secure and extensively used symmetric encryption algorithms in contemporary cybersecurity. Standardized by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), AES operates as a symmetric block cipher capable of encrypting and decrypting data using key sizes of 128, 192, or 256 bits [123]. This design enables AES to secure sensitive information across a wide range of applications [123], [124].

Encryption protects confidentiality by transforming plaintext into ciphertext that cannot be interpreted without the correct cryptographic key. In cloud environments, encryption may be applied either at the client side or the server side. Client-side encryption, where data is encrypted before transmission to the cloud, is often considered more secure because it reduces exposure to external threats and potential service-provider vulnerabilities. AES replaced the earlier Data Encryption Standard due to its stronger key lengths and improved resistance to brute-force and cryptanalytic attacks. Because of its security strength and computational efficiency, AES is commonly recommended for protecting both data-at-rest and data-in-transit in cloud storage systems [59], [60].

#### 2.1.3.2 LIMITATIONS OF ENCRYPTION AND THE IMPORTANCE OF KEY MANAGEMENT
Despite its effectiveness, encryption alone cannot eliminate all security risks. Encrypted systems may still be vulnerable to attacks such as side-channel attacks, cryptographic implementation flaws, and brute-force attempts. Security analyses show that even mathematically strong encryption algorithms can be compromised through physical information leakage, including variations in power consumption, processing time, or electromagnetic emissions during cryptographic operations [61]. These findings highlight that the overall security of encrypted systems depends not only on the algorithm itself but also on how it is implemented and deployed.

Furthermore, the effectiveness of encryption is highly dependent on proper cryptographic key management. Research emphasizes that weak key management practices can undermine the theoretical security of encryption algorithms such as AES. If encryption keys are exposed, improperly stored, or poorly generated, the protection provided by encryption can be completely negated. Consequently, secure key management practices that include key generation, distribution, storage, and rotation are essential for maintaining the confidentiality and integrity of protected data [62], [63].

### 2.1.4 KEY DERIVATION FUNCTIONS AND KEY MANAGEMENT

#### 2.1.4.1 KEY DERIVATION FUNCTIONS FOR SECURE KEY GENERATION
In encryption-based systems, effective key management is essential for maintaining overall security. One widely used mechanism for strengthening cryptographic keys is the Key Derivation Function (KDF), which derives secure keys from passwords or shared secrets. Commonly adopted KDFs include PBKDF2 and HKDF, both of which enhance password-based encryption by incorporating salting mechanisms and configurable computational costs [125], [126]. These features significantly increase resistance to brute-force and dictionary attacks [125] .

Recent research highlights the evolving role of KDFs in modern security architectures. Abdullah proposed an adaptive model of PBKDF2 that dynamically adjusts iteration counts according to contextual factors such as password strength and device capability. By increasing computational cost when necessary, the model improves resilience against offline brute-force attacks while maintaining system performance. Similarly, formal cryptographic analysis by Hoang and colleagues demonstrates that HKDF produces secure pseudorandom outputs suitable for cryptographic key generation when the initial keying material or salt contains sufficient randomness [64], [65]. The theoretical basis of HKDF originates from the work of Hugo Krawczyk, who introduced the extract-then-expand paradigm, explaining how entropy can be securely extracted from weak input material and expanded into strong cryptographic keys. This framework has since been widely adopted in modern cryptographic standards [66].

Practical implementations further support these findings. For example, the Linux Unified Key Setup utilizes PBKDF2 with salting and configurable iteration counts to strengthen disk encryption. Increasing the number of iterations significantly raises the computational cost of brute-force and dictionary attacks while maintaining usability for legitimate users [67].

#### 2.1.4.2 IMPORTANCE OF CRYPTOGRAPHIC KEY MANAGEMENT
Despite the strength of modern encryption algorithms, improper key management remains a major source of security vulnerabilities. Studies indicate that weak key storage practices, inadequate access control mechanisms, and infrequent key rotation can compromise encrypted systems regardless of the algorithm used. As a result, effective key management practices including secure key generation, distribution, storage, and periodic rotation are critical components of secure document management systems [68].

Integrating KDF-based key generation with controlled authorization mechanisms can further enhance system security. By adjusting computational workload based on contextual factors such as device capability and data sensitivity, adaptive KDF implementations strengthen derived keys when higher protection is required while minimizing overhead in lower-risk scenarios. This approach helps balance security and usability while improving resistance to password-guessing and brute-force attacks [64], [65].

### 2.1.5 STEGANOGRAPHY IN DIGITAL SECURITY
Steganography refers to the practice of concealing information within a non-secret file or medium in order to obscure the very existence of communication. Unlike encryption, which protects the content of data, steganography hides the presence of the message itself. Recent digital steganography studies demonstrate that various media types — including text, audio, and images — can serve as cover objects for embedding hidden information [69], [70]. One commonly used technique is the modification of least significant bits (LSBs) in digital data, such as pixel values in images or sample values in audio files. Through altering these LSBs, secret bits can be embedded in a way that remains imperceptible to human users while still encoding concealed information [70].

To further strengthen security, crypto-steganography systems integrate encryption with steganographic techniques [127]. In this layered approach, data is first encrypted and then embedded within a cover medium [128]. This dual-protection mechanism enhances both confidentiality and concealment: even if the hidden data is detected or extracted, it remains encrypted and unreadable without the appropriate cryptographic key. Such integration provides an additional security layer beyond traditional encryption or steganography alone, reinforcing protection against interception and unauthorized access [127] , [129].

Despite its advantages, steganographic systems are subject to several technical limitations. Embedding large encrypted payloads can degrade the quality of the cover media and reduce overall system efficiency. Commonly cited challenges include limited payload capacity and increased computational overhead, particularly when advanced embedding techniques are employed. A 2025 review published in Scientific Reports analyzes modern steganographic approaches and identifies constraints such as low embedding capacity and increased computational complexity, especially in deep-learning-based methods [71]. Although these advanced techniques may enhance security and robustness, they often introduce heavier processing requirements and difficulties in balancing payload size, imperceptibility, and resistance to detection.

Additional peer-reviewed studies further confirm that increasing payload size tends to negatively affect the perceptual quality of stego media, reinforcing a fundamental trade-off in many embedding strategies [71]. These findings highlight that steganographic capacity is inherently constrained by the size of the cover medium and the need to preserve visual or auditory quality. Consequently, achieving an optimal balance between security, capacity, efficiency, and imperceptibility remains a central challenge in steganographic system design.

### 2.1.6 ENCRYPTED FILE SEGMENTATION AND MULTI-LOCATION CLOUD STORAGE

#### 2.1.6.1 ENCRYPTED FILE SEGMENTATION AS A SECURITY MECHANISM
In secure document storage systems, segmentation refers to the deliberate splitting of an already-encrypted file into multiple separate parts referred to here as segments prior to storage. This approach is distinct from encryption itself: while encryption protects the content of data by rendering it unreadable without a key, segmentation addresses a different threat ensuring that no single stored file contains enough information to be useful to an attacker, even if the file is obtained. A file-splitting approach for cloud data security distributes encrypted file segments across multiple cloud service providers, significantly reducing the likelihood of unauthorized access, as each individual segment in isolation contains insufficient information for reconstruction [50].

This principle is formalized in cryptographic literature through the concept of secret sharing — also called secret splitting — wherein a secret is divided such that no individual piece reveals any intelligible information on its own. Secret sharing distributes a secret among a group such that no individual holds any intelligible information about the secret, but when a sufficient number of individuals combine their shares, the secret may be reconstructed. Secure secret sharing is effectively "all or nothing" a single share is entirely useless without the others. Waseem et al. (2025) demonstrate that splitting a file into encrypted segments dispersed across multiple cloud providers precludes unauthorized access by a single provider, as authenticated access and reassembly of all segments are required for secure recovery meaning no individual segment yields recoverable content on its own [98].

As validated in [74], threshold secret sharing provides mathematical certainty that no individual share reveals any information about the original secret on its own, meaning that an attacker who obtains a portion of segments gains no meaningful data unless they acquire all segments required to meet the reconstruction threshold. This structural guarantee is what distinguishes segmentation as a security mechanism from conventional single-file encrypted storage. Supporting this, practical implementations of secret sharing in cloud storage systems demonstrate that cryptographic keys can be divided into shares distributed across separate cloud locations, such that each location holds only one share improving the security of sensitive data because no single cloud provider can access the complete secret [97].

#### 2.1.6.2 MULTI-LOCATION STORAGE OF ENCRYPTED SEGMENTS
Once an encrypted file is split into segments, distributing those segments across separate, independent cloud storage locations introduces an additional structural security layer [79], [50]. This is not the same as distributed computing infrastructure — the concept here is straightforward: each encrypted segment is stored as a separate file in a distinct cloud location, so that no single location holds the complete encrypted file [130]. The security benefit is architectural: even if one storage location is compromised, the attacker retrieves only one segment, which carries no reconstructible content without the remaining pieces [130], [50].

Frameworks that split data into chunks and encrypt each portion separately demonstrate that this approach strengthens user-side data security — combining the protection of encryption with the structural protection of splitting, ensuring that the compromise of any single chunk does not expose the full original data [100]. This is directly aligned with the architecture of the proposed system, wherein each encrypted segment is embedded within a steganographic carrier file and stored at a separate cloud location meaning an adversary must successfully retrieve all carrier files, extract all embedded segments, and reassemble them before any decryption attempt can occur.

The security value of this multi-location approach is further supported by Tang and Li [79], who propose a multi-layer secure cloud storage model that includes secure data partitioning and dispersed storage across several cloud platforms, improving privacy-preserving capabilities in data-sensitive environments. Similarly, Sudha and Ramesh [78] establish that integrating file splitting, encryption, and access control mechanisms adds a meaningful security layer with insignificant system overhead which the security and confidentiality benefits far outweigh the computational costs introduced. Collectively, these works confirm that splitting an encrypted file across multiple independent storage locations reduces reliance solely on cryptographic strength and incorporates structural protection mechanisms, a principle central to the reconstruction-dependent security model of the proposed system.

### 2.1.7 RECONSTRUCTION-DEPENDENT SECURITY MODELS

#### 2.1.7.1 ASSEMBLY-DEPENDENT DECRYPTION AS A CORE SECURITY PRINCIPLE
Reconstruction-dependent security extends beyond conventional encryption by requiring the structural assembly of segmented encrypted components as a mandatory precondition for decryption. According to the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), in attribute-based encryption systems, decryption is only possible when the access policy conditions built into the ciphertext structure are fully satisfied — meaning isolated ciphertext components alone are insufficient for decryption without the correct structural conditions being met [80]. This demonstrates that modern cryptographic architectures can enforce assembly-dependent decryption as a core security mechanism, where access to ciphertext alone confers no meaningful advantage to an adversary.

This principle is corroborated by Abusukhon et al. [81], whose study published in Scientific Reports confirms that even when an attacker obtains encrypted data, decryption remains impossible unless all required structural components satisfy the access policy — establishing that possession of ciphertext alone does not constitute meaningful access to protected information. The mathematical formalization of this requirement is provided by the NIST Multi-Party Threshold Cryptography Project (2020), which establishes that a cryptographic operation such as decryption can only be completed when a sufficient number of parties or segments — exceeding a defined threshold — contribute their shares collectively. Critically, any group smaller than the threshold cannot determine the secret even through collaboration, and reconstruction must occur before decryption proceeds [78]. Yu et al. [83], in their study on adaptively secure threshold unsigncryption published in Cybersecurity (SpringerOpen), further confirm that threshold cryptography divides decryption responsibilities such that a minimum required number of parties must cooperate to achieve successful decryption — improving both security and resilience against adversarial scenarios.

#### 2.1.7.2 THE THREE-BARRIER SECURITY ARCHITECTURE
The reconstruction-dependent model introduces three sequential and interdependent security barriers that collectively govern access to protected data [78], [73].

The first is the **Segmentation Barrier**, in which the encrypted file is split into multiple separate segments prior to storage. No individual segment contains sufficient data to reconstruct the original file [98] , [50]. Waseem et al. [98] demonstrate that splitting a file into encrypted segments and dispersing them across multiple storage locations precludes unauthorized access by any single party, as all segments must be retrieved and reassembled for secure recovery — meaning no individual segment yields recoverable content on its own.

The second is the **Distribution Barrier**, in which each segment is embedded into a separate steganographic carrier file and stored at a distinct cloud location.As established in [84], embedding encrypted data into image carriers distributed across separate storage locations substantially reduces the risk of data loss, manipulation, and theft — because no single storage location holds the complete dataset, and the concealment layer makes the presence of hidden data imperceptible to external observers.

The third is the **Reconstruction Barrier**, which enforces that all distributed carrier files must be successfully retrieved and their segments reassembled before any decryption operation can be initiated. As demonstrated in [90], the crypto-steganography approach enforces mandatory retrieval and reassembly as a precondition of decryption — meaning an attacker who obtains only one carrier file cannot reconstruct or decrypt the original data without acquiring all remaining segments and proper authorization credentials.

#### 2.1.7.3 COMBINED ARCHITECTURE AND DEFENSE-IN-DEPTH PRINCIPLES
Through combining cryptography, steganography, and file segmentation, this reconstruction-dependent architecture mitigates the risks associated with ciphertext exfiltration.As demonstrated in [97], systems that segment and encrypt data while concealing decryption keys within steganographic carriers ensure that even if ciphertext is exfiltrated, it remains inaccessible without the corresponding carrier file containing the embedded key. This binding of ciphertext and key carrier across separate, distributed files is precisely what renders single-file exfiltration attacks ineffective against this architecture.

This layered model reflects the well-established principle of defense-in-depth, wherein multiple independent security controls are stacked such that the failure of any one layer does not result in total system compromise. Defense-in-depth is a multi-layered cybersecurity approach wherein each independent control layer functions separately which ensures that if one defense is breached, the remaining measures continue to contain and mitigate the threat [87]. Fitzgerald and Morris [88] further confirm that combining multiple security layers improves threat detection, response, and overall system resilience against adversarial conditions. This is reinforced by Godakanda [89], whose work on defense-in-depth modeling confirms that architectures incorporating multiple independent barrier layers significantly reduce exploitable attack surfaces [85]. The segmentation–distribution–reconstruction architecture of the proposed system directly embodies these principles: each barrier operates independently, and all three must be overcome sequentially for an adversary to access the protected document.

### 2.1.8 USABILITY CONSIDERATIONS IN SECURE WEB APPLICATIONS

#### 2.1.8.1 THE SECURITY-USABILITY TRADEOFF
Security and usability are frequently positioned as competing objectives in the design of secure digital systems. More secure systems tend to be less usable, while more usable systems often sacrifice security — a tension that complicates the design of systems that must satisfy both requirements simultaneously [91]. This tradeoff is corroborated by a systematic review of usable security research spanning nearly two decades, which confirms that this inverse relationship between security strength and usability is a consistent and well-documented finding across the field [92].

The practical consequences of this tension are significant. Braun et al. demonstrate that overly complex security workflows such as those requiring users to manage cryptographic credentials manually that frequently result in risky compensatory behaviors, including password reuse and insecure credential storage [93]. This means that a system designed with strong cryptographic protections can paradoxically become less secure in practice if its interface places excessive cognitive demands on users. Ma et al. [94] reinforce this by establishing that effective hybrid encryption architectures must be designed for practical deployability alongside cryptographic robustness just simplifying workflows and ensuring cross-platform compatibility without exposing sensitive data to insecure handling.

#### 2.1.8.2 USABILITY EVALUATION AND LIGHTWEIGHT DESIGN PRINCIPLES
Given the security-usability tradeoff, formal evaluation of user experience is an essential component of secure system development. The System Usability Scale (SUS) is among the most widely adopted instruments for this purpose. Deshmukh and Chalmeta [72] confirm that SUS can be applied across all types of interactive digital platforms including web applications, mobile systems, and expert tools — making it a suitable evaluation instrument for security-integrated systems where user experience directly influences adoption and compliance behavior.

On the design side, Mamun et al. [90] demonstrate that lightweight key management structures simplify key lifecycle processes — including allocation, distribution, and rotation — while simultaneously reducing the computational complexity of encryption and decryption operations, resulting in faster processing and a more accessible user experience. This is complemented by Krähenbühl and Perrig [91], who affirm that simplifying key management for end users is not merely a usability concern but a security one — poorly managed keys represent one of the most persistent vulnerabilities in cryptographic systems regardless of algorithm strength.

Collectively, these findings establish that usability must be treated as an equally integral dimension of secure web application design. In the context of StegoLock, where the security architecture involves multiple sequential operations – encryption, steganographic embedding, segmentation, and cloud storage ensuring that these processes are streamlined and transparent to the user is critical to both practical adoption and the integrity of the security model itself.

### 2.1.9 SYNTHESIS OF REVIEWED LITERATURE
The literature reviewed across the preceding sections does not present isolated topics , rather it traces a logical progression in which each mechanism addresses a specific limitation left unresolved by the one before it.

**Encryption** establishes the baseline: it renders data unreadable without a key. However, as the reviewed literature consistently demonstrates, encryption's protection collapses the moment its key is compromised through brute-force attacks, side-channel leakage, or poor key management practices. **Key Derivation Functions** respond to this weakness not by changing the encryption model but by hardening the process through which keys are generated and strengthened. Both mechanisms, however, operate entirely within the same layer: they protect data content but leave the existence and location of that data fully exposed.

On the other hand, **Steganography** operates on a fundamentally different dimension. Rather than making data unreadable, it makes data undetectable. This is a qualitative shift — an attacker who does not know protected data exists has no target to pursue. When combined with encryption as crypto-steganography, both the content and the presence of data are simultaneously protected. Yet even this combination shares a critical structural vulnerability: the entire protected payload resides in a single carrier file. Obtaining that one file is sufficient to attempt decryption or steganalysis.

**Encrypted file segmentation** directly addresses this single-file vulnerability by ensuring that no individual stored piece contains sufficient information for reconstruction. This introduces a structural guarantee that neither encryption, key hardening, nor steganography can provide on their own. **Multi-location storage** then extends this guarantee spatially , no single storage location holds a complete dataset, meaning compromise of any one location yields nothing actionable. Together, segmentation and multi-location storage shift security from a content-based model to an architecture-based one.

**Reconstruction-dependent security** is where these layers converge into a unified access model. It formalizes what the preceding mechanisms collectively imply: that decryption is not merely key-protected but architecturally prevented until every segment is retrieved, reassembled, and authorized. This transforms independent controls into a sequential pipeline which an adversary must defeat every layer, in order, to gain any access.

**Usability** sits across all of these layers as a transversal concern. Unlike the other mechanisms, it does not add a security barrier instead, it determines whether the barriers that exist are actually used correctly. A system whose security depends on proper user behavior, such as careful key handling or multi-step retrieval, is only as strong as its interface allows users to be. This is why usability is not peripheral to StegoLock's design but integral to it: the system's multi-layer architecture is only effective if users can operate it consistently and correctly.

### 2.2.1 CLOUD-BASED DOCUMENT STORAGE APPLICATIONS
Google Drive, Dropbox, and Microsoft OneDrive are among the most widely used cloud storage platforms globally. These three platforms consistently rank among the top cloud storage services by active user base and enterprise adoption, making them the primary reference points for evaluating cloud document storage security [101]. Their relevance to StegoLock lies in their shared purpose which is storing and retrieving digital documents through a web-based interface which is the same core function that StegoLock is designed to serve.

#### 2.2.1.1 GOOGLE DRIVE
Google Drive is a cloud-based file storage and synchronization service developed by Alphabet Inc. It provides users with web-based access to stored documents, supporting real-time collaboration and cross-device synchronization. The platform secures stored data using AES-256 encryption at rest and TLS encryption in transit, managed entirely by Google's server-side infrastructure. Access is controlled through Google account credentials and optional multi-factor authentication [100].

#### 2.2.1.2 DROPBOX
Dropbox is a cloud file hosting service that allows users to store, access, and share documents through a web and desktop interface. It applies AES-256 encryption for data at rest and SSL/TLS for data in transit, with encryption managed by Dropbox's servers. The platform supports OAuth-based third-party integrations and account-level access controls. A 2022 security incident involving Dropbox demonstrated that a successful phishing attack against an employee can bypass multi-factor authentication entirely, resulting in unauthorized access to internal repositories illustrating the limitations of authentication-layer-only protection [98].

#### 2.2.1.3 MICROSOFT ONEDRIVE
Microsoft OneDrive is a cloud storage service integrated into the Microsoft 365 ecosystem, offering document storage, synchronization, and sharing capabilities through a web interface. It employs AES-256 encryption at rest and TLS in transit, with encryption managed by Microsoft's infrastructure. Access is governed through Microsoft account credentials, role-based permissions, and OAuth-based application integrations [103]. A documented OAuth scope vulnerability in 2025 exposed a design limitation wherein third-party applications were granted broader data access than explicitly authorized by users [102].

### 2.2.2 CRYPTO-STEGANOGRAPHIC SECURITY SYSTEMS

The following systems combine encryption and steganography as complementary security mechanisms. Their relevance to StegoLock lies in their shared use of cryptographic and steganographic techniques for data protection, which constitutes the foundational security approach of StegoLock.

#### 2.2.2.1 4-STEP CRYPTO-STEGO CLOUD MODEL
This system, published in MDPI Sensors, presents a four-step data security model for cloud computing that sequentially applies RSA encryption, AES encryption, identity-based encryption, and LSB steganographic embedding into image carriers. Encrypted data is stored in the cloud with the stego-image serving as the access artifact. The system demonstrates that layering cryptographic and steganographic techniques provides stronger protection than encryption alone, particularly against unauthorized access during storage [95].

#### 2.2.2.2 CLOUD CRYPTO-SYSTEM SYSTEM
Proposed in the Journal La Multiapp, this system conceals encrypted data and its corresponding decryption key within steganographic image carriers stored in cloud environments. A mandatory key retrieval step from the stego-image is required before decryption can proceed, introducing a partial form of reconstruction dependency [101]. The system operates as a standalone implementation without a dedicated web interface for end users.

#### 2.2.2.3 CryptSteg
Published by IEEE, CryptSteg integrates ciphertext policy-attribute-based encryption (CP-ABE) with multi-cover image steganography. Ciphertext payloads are distributed across multiple steganographic cover images, and decryption is conditioned on satisfying the embedded access policy implementing both multi-location distribution and a policy-enforced reconstruction requirement [99]. The system's use of CP-ABE, however, introduces significant computational overhead and does not include a simplified web deployment or formal usability evaluation.

#### 2.2.2.4 AES STEGANOGRAPHY + BLOCKCHAIN
Published in Informatica, this system combines AES encryption with LSB steganography and integrates blockchain technology to provide immutable audit trails and tamper detection for encrypted image carriers stored in the cloud [105]. The blockchain layer adds verifiability to the storage process. However, the system does not implement file segmentation across multiple stego-carriers, does not enforce reconstruction-dependent decryption, and the blockchain dependency adds infrastructure overhead that limits lightweight web deployment.

### 2.2.3 WEB-BASED STEGANOGRAPHIC AND SECURE DOCUMENT APPLICATIONS

The following systems are relevant to StegoLock in terms of their deployment context could either as web-based applications or as systems designed specifically for securing digital documents. Their relevance establishes that steganographic techniques can be effectively deployed in web environments and applied to document-format files.

#### 2.2.3.1 STEGANOGRAPHY IN WEB APPLICATIONS
Published in the Journal of Information Systems Engineering and Management, this system implements SVM-based pixel priority LSB embedding within a browser-accessible web application, achieving a PSNR of 87 dB [100]. It demonstrates that steganographic embedding can be successfully deployed through a web interface while maintaining imperceptibility. The system does not integrate encryption alongside steganography and does not implement file segmentation or cloud-based multi-location storage. Usability is partially assessed through task-based evaluation metrics.

#### 2.2.3.2 PDF CRYPTO-STEGANOGRAPHY SYSTEM
Published in Springer's IoT-Based Control Networks and Intelligent Systems, this system embeds AES-256 and RSA-encrypted data within PDF documents without visually altering the document's content [104]. It demonstrates the feasibility of applying crypto-steganographic techniques directly to document-format files — the same file type that StegoLock targets as its primary storage artifact. The system operates as a desktop application, does not distribute stego-files across multiple cloud locations, and does not enforce reconstruction-dependent decryption.

| **ES8** | None | SVM-based pixel priority LSB | High imperceptibility, accessible via browser | Lacks encryption, segmentation, and multi-location storage |
| **ES9** | AES-256, RSA | PDF document embedding | Direct application to document-format files | Desktop only, lacks cloud distribution and reconstruction |

### 2.2.4 SYSTEM COMPARISON AND ANALYSIS

The existing systems reviewed in this section present varying objectives and capabilities, each addressing distinct security and operational requirements. To facilitate a structured comparison, these platforms are designated as Existing Systems (ES) 1 through 9. Their capabilities are evaluated across two primary domains, represented in Table 1 and Table 2.

**Domain 1: Operational and Deployment Characteristics**
- **CAT1 (Primary Function):** The core purpose and operational objective of the system.
- **CAT2 (Access Control):** The mechanisms governing user authentication and data access.
- **CAT3 (Web Interface):** The availability of a browser-based deployment model for end-user accessibility.

**Domain 2: Cryptographic and Security Characteristics**
- **CAT4 (Cryptography Used):** The specific encryption algorithms and cryptographic standards employed.
- **CAT5 (Steganography Method):** The techniques used to conceal data within a carrier medium.
- **CAT6 (Security Strengths):** The primary defensive advantages and architectural benefits.
- **CAT7 (Security Weaknesses):** The identified vulnerabilities, structural limitations, or operational risks.

Cloud-based document storage platforms, including ES1 (Google Drive), ES2 (Dropbox), and ES3 (Microsoft OneDrive), share a primary function of storing, synchronizing, and sharing digital documents. These systems excel in their deployment context, providing robust web interfaces, strong provider-managed AES encryption, and scalable multi-location cloud infrastructures. Their primary strength lies in their accessibility and collaborative capabilities. However, a comparative evaluation highlights a shared architectural limitation: their security models are fundamentally reliant on perimeter authentication. Once access credentials are compromised, stored data becomes fully accessible since there is no structural barrier or steganographic concealment to protect the data independently of the platform's access controls.

In contrast, crypto-steganographic systems prioritize data obfuscation and layered protection over seamless collaboration. Systems like ES4 (4-Step Crypto-Stego Cloud Model), ES5 (Cloud Crypto-System System), and ES7 (AES Steganography + Blockchain) successfully demonstrate that integrating encryption with steganographic embedding provides a stronger defense against unauthorized data extraction. ES4 and ES5 leverage image carriers to hide encrypted data, while ES7 introduces blockchain for tamper detection. However, these systems generally suffer from single-carrier vulnerabilities, meaning the entirety of the payload resides in one stego-file. If an adversary acquires this single file, they possess all the necessary data to attempt decryption. ES6 (CryptSteg) addresses this specific weakness by distributing ciphertext across multiple covers and enforcing policy-based reconstruction, but its reliance on heavy attribute-based encryption introduces significant computational overhead that hinders lightweight deployment. Furthermore, none of ES4, ES5, ES6, or ES7 provide a web interface, limiting their practical usability for end users.

Finally, systems focused on deployment flexibility demonstrate the feasibility of bringing advanced steganography to more accessible environments. ES8 (Steganography in Web Applications) successfully implements LSB embedding through a browser interface with high imperceptibility, though it entirely omits encryption and segmentation, rendering it unsuited for high-security applications. Meanwhile, ES9 (PDF Crypto-Steganography System) proves that crypto-steganography can be effectively applied directly to document-format files. Nevertheless, ES9 remains restricted to a desktop environment, lacking both the multi-location distribution of cloud platforms and the accessibility of web applications.

By comparing these systems, it becomes evident that while individual platforms successfully achieve specific objectives—such as the usability of ES1, ES2, and ES3, the layered concealment of ES4, ES5, and ES7, or the deployment innovations of ES8 and ES9—none offer a comprehensive integration of these capabilities. This analysis emphasizes the need for an architecture that combines the accessibility of web-based cloud storage with the robust protection of segmented, reconstruction-dependent crypto-steganography.
